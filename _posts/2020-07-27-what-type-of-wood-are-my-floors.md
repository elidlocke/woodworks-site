Most hardwood floors in Southern Ontario homes are red oak, but I also see plenty of maple, pine, birch and the odd exotic like Brazilian cherry. Knowing which one you have tells you how well it'll stand up to kids and dogs, how much it'll yellow over the years, and which stain colours will actually look good on it.

I get asked this a lot when I come out to quote a job. The age of the house is usually the first clue. A 1910 farmhouse in Cambridge, a 1960s bungalow in Kitchener and a 2005 build in Waterloo will almost always have different floors. Here's how to tell what's under your feet, and what that means when it's time to refinish.

## How hard is your floor? The Janka scale

The flooring industry rates hardness with the Janka test: it measures the force needed to push a small steel ball halfway into a piece of wood. The higher the number, the better the floor resists dents from dropped pots, high heels and dog claws. Red oak, at about 1,290 lbf, is the benchmark most people compare against.

Hardness numbers are typical published Janka values and vary a bit from source to source. How fast a floor yellows also depends on the finish: oil-based polyurethane ambers every wood, while a water-based finish keeps it much closer to its natural colour.

## How to tell what you've got

### Pine (red and white)

Pine is what you'll find in a lot of century homes and old farmhouses around the region, often in wide boards nailed straight to the joists. It's a softwood, and it shows: white pine is about a third as hard as oak, so it's covered in dents, scratches and character marks. Look for big, dark, round knots and wide, soft grain with strong contrast between the light and dark bands.

Pine yellows more than any other floor I work on. A freshly sanded pine floor looks pale and creamy, but within a few years under an oil-based finish it turns deep yellow-orange. Some people love that warm, old-house look. If you don't, a water-based finish slows it down a lot.

### Red oak

If your house was built between the 1940s and today, there's a good chance it's red oak, usually in 2 1/4" strips. It has an open, wavy "cathedral" grain and a warm, slightly pinkish tone. It's the middle of the road for hardness, which is a big part of why it's everywhere. Red oak ambers to a golden-orange over time, especially under oil-based finishes.

### White oak

White oak looks similar to red oak, but it's more olive-brown than pink, with straighter grain and little flecks or ribbons in the boards (called ray fleck). It's a bit harder than red oak and changes colour less, which is why it's the go-to for the light, natural and grey looks that are popular right now.

### Maple

Maple is very pale, almost cream, with a tight, smooth grain and hardly any visible pores. It's a lot harder than pine and a step up from oak, which is why it's used on gym and bowling alley floors. I see it most in homes from the 1970s through the 1990s. Maple yellows noticeably under an oil-based finish, turning from white to a honey-yellow.

### Birch

Birch is common in Canadian-made strip flooring and gets mistaken for maple all the time. It's a little softer than maple, with more colour variation from board to board, often with some reddish-brown heartwood mixed in with the pale sapwood. Like maple, it yellows to a honey colour over time.

### Walnut

Walnut is a rich chocolate brown, sometimes with purple tones, and you'll usually find it in higher-end homes or as a feature border. It's noticeably softer than oak, so it dents more easily. Walnut is unusual because it gets lighter over time instead of darker, mellowing into a softer, warmer brown.

### Brazilian cherry (Jatoba)

Brazilian cherry was the exotic floor of choice from the late 1990s through the 2000s. It's extremely hard, nearly twice as hard as red oak, so it holds up very well. Fresh boards are salmon-pink to orange, but they darken dramatically in sunlight to a deep red-brown, often within the first year. You can usually see lighter "shadows" where a rug or furniture sat.

### Others you might find

- **American cherry** is softer than oak and darkens from pinkish to a rich reddish-brown. It's mostly in older custom homes or inlays.
- **Ash** looks like a lighter, straighter-grained oak and holds up about as well.
- **Hickory** is very hard, with wild colour variation from cream to dark brown in the same floor.
- **Douglas fir** shows up in some pre-1940 homes, often upstairs. It's soft like pine and yellows to orange.

## Which stain colours work on which wood

Oak takes almost any stain well; maple, birch, pine and Brazilian cherry are pickier. Open-grained woods soak up stain evenly, while tight-grained woods tend to go blotchy, especially in darker colours. I always lay down test patches on your actual floor before we pick, because the same stain can look completely different from one species to the next.

<div class="table-responsive" markdown="1">

| Wood | Takes stain | Colours I'd recommend | What I'd steer away from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Red oak | Very well | Natural, warm medium browns, rich dark walnut, espresso | Cool greys, which can pick up the wood's pink tone |
| White oak | Very well | Natural, light "Scandinavian" neutrals, greys, whitewash, smoky browns, ebony | Very little; it's the most flexible floor there is |
| Ash | Very well | Natural, light neutrals, whitewash, medium browns | Heavy reds |
| Hickory | Fairly well | Natural, light to medium browns | Dark stains that hide the grain contrast |
| Maple | Blotchy | Natural with Bona NordicSeal or Traffic HD, very light neutrals | Medium and dark stains unless the wood is specially prepped first |
| Birch | Blotchy | Natural, light honey tones | Dark stains |
| Pine and fir | Blotchy | Natural, warm golden or light brown tones, a soft whitewash | Dark stains, which go patchy and highlight every dent |
| Walnut | Doesn't need it | Natural, clear finish only | Any stain; it hides what you paid for |
| Brazilian cherry | Poorly (oily, dense) | Natural, clear finish only | Anything lighter; very dark stains only with careful testing |
| American cherry | Fair | Natural, clear finish only | Dark stains; it darkens on its own |

</div>

Want a floor that changes colour completely, like going from orange-toned oak to a modern grey? Oak can do that. Brazilian cherry and walnut can't, so it's better to love them as they are.

### Stopping the yellow with natural finishes

If you want a pale floor to stay naturally white, the finish matters more than the stain. Oil-based polyurethane ambers every wood a little more each year. These Bona water-based products keep a floor much closer to the colour you see on the day it's done:

- **Bona Traffic HD** is the clear, commercial-grade finish I use most. It doesn't amber the way oil-based finishes do, so it's my pick for maple, birch, white oak and pine.
- **Bona NordicSeal** is a sealer that goes on before the finish. It counteracts the natural yellow tone in light woods and gives a soft, Scandinavian look. It works best on white oak, ash and maple.
- **Bona Traffic Naturale** is an ultra-matte finish that makes the floor look like raw, unfinished wood. It's great on white oak and ash if you want that natural, no-shine look.

### Whitewashing

A whitewash is a white stain that lets the grain show through. It looks best on open-grained woods that hold the white pigment in their pores:

- **White oak and ash** take a whitewash best, for a clean, beachy or Scandinavian look.
- **Red oak** works too, but it comes out a warmer, slightly pinkish white.
- **Pine** gives a classic cottage look. Expect the knots to show through.

I'd skip a whitewash on maple and birch, because the tight grain doesn't hold the pigment evenly and it goes blotchy. Walnut and both cherries are too dark for white to look right. Whatever wood we whitewash, it always gets a water-based topcoat like Traffic HD, because an oil-based finish will turn the white yellow.
