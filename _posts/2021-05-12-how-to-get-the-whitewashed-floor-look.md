You've probably seen them in magazines and all over Instagram. Bright Scandinavian homes with pale, soft, almost-raw-looking oak floors. And you thought, "I want that." You're not alone. It's one of the most popular looks out there right now, and the product I use most to get it is Bona NordicSeal. This isn't the pinkish pickled oak of the 90s. It's softer and more natural.

I'll be honest up front. A whitewashed floor is less forgiving to put down than a natural finish or a regular stain. There's less room for error, and it takes more care on my end. But when it's done right, it gives a floor a clean, timeless look that holds up for years.

There are also some interesting products out there that colour and protect the floor in one step, without a separate topcoat. They get you to a similar look, but they work in a completely different way. Here's how they compare, and how to decide which one suits your home.

## What is Bona NordicSeal?

[Bona NordicSeal](https://ardec.ca/en/p/1205/bona-nordicseal-waterborne-wood-floor-sealer) is a water-based sealer with a little white pigment in it. It goes on the bare wood right after sanding, before the finish. The white softens the natural yellow and orange tones in the wood, so the floor looks pale and close to raw, instead of golden.

It's not a stain, and it's not a finish on its own. It's the first layer of a system. Here's how I put it down:

1. Sand the floor all the way to bare wood, and finish with a fine, even final sanding.
2. Vacuum and tack the floor so there's no dust left.
3. Apply one coat of NordicSeal. One coat gives a soft whitewashed look. A second coat makes it whiter.
4. Apply two coats of a Bona water-based finish over top. I usually use Bona Traffic HD, and Traffic Naturale if you want an ultra-matte, raw-wood look.

That's three or four coats in all. The white is sealed under a hard, clear topcoat, and that topcoat is what takes the wear. A few things to know:

- **It needs a water-based topcoat.** NordicSeal doesn't yellow, but an oil-based finish over it will turn the white yellow.
- **It doesn't go over stain.** It's a pigmented sealer, so it goes straight onto bare wood.
- **It suits some woods more than others.** It looks best on white oak, ash and maple. Red oak comes out warmer and a little pinkish. If you're not sure what you have, [here's how to tell](/what-type-of-wood-are-my-floors).

Because the colour sits under the topcoat, a NordicSeal floor can be [buffed and recoated](/hardwood-floor-buff-and-coat/) down the road without losing the white. The look you pay for is the look you keep.

## The other way: oils that colour and protect in one step

There's a whole family of products that skip the topcoat altogether. They're usually called hardwax oils or penetrating oils. Instead of building a clear film on top of the wood, they soak into the wood fibres and harden there. Many come in white or pale tints, so they colour and protect the floor at the same time.

Here are a few you'll see, from different brands:

- **[Rubio Monocoat Oil Plus 2C](https://ardec.ca/en/p/1127/rubio-monocoat-oil-plus-2c)**, in shades like White 5% or Pure. It's made to go on in a single coat. The oil bonds to the top of the wood fibres, and the extra gets wiped off. It's a two-part product, with an accelerator that speeds up the cure.
- **[Osmo Polyx-Oil Tints](https://osmouk.com/product/polyx-oil-tints/)**, like 3040 White. Osmo says only one coat of tint goes on a floor, and that it shouldn't be topped with clear Polyx-Oil, which can yellow the white.
- **WOCA Master Colour Oil**, a Danish oil that comes in white and pale tints. It's cared for with WOCA's own soaps, including a white version made to keep white-oiled floors looking white.
- **[Bona Craft Oil 2K](https://ardec.ca/en/p/1298/bona-craft-oil-2k-huile-naturelle-penetrante-pour)**, in Frost. Even Bona makes an oil. Frost is a neutral with a hint of warm white, and it's a two-part oil mixed with a hardener.

### Why it's a different process

A sealer and topcoat like NordicSeal builds layers that sit on top of the wood. An oil goes into the wood. That changes almost everything about how the floor is put down, and how it's looked after:

- **How it goes on.** An oil is worked into the wood with a pad or buffer, and the extra is wiped off within minutes. Nothing is left sitting on the surface. With NordicSeal, every coat stays on the floor.
- **How it feels.** An oiled floor feels like wood. A NordicSeal floor has a thin, smooth film of finish over the wood, even in a matte sheen.
- **How it wears.** With a topcoat, traffic wears the finish, not the colour. With an oil, the colour is in the wood surface itself, so busy paths can lose some of their white over time.
- **How it's repaired.** This is where oils shine. A worn or scratched spot can be cleaned and re-oiled on its own, and it blends right in. A film finish usually has to be buffed and recoated across the whole room.
- **How it's maintained.** Oiled floors need regular care: cleaning with the brand's own soap, and a fresh coat of maintenance oil every so often. A NordicSeal floor gets a regular hardwood cleaner, and a recoat every several years.

## Why a whitewashed floor is less forgiving

With a natural finish, a clear coat goes over bare wood, and small differences disappear into the grain. A medium or dark stain hides a lot too. White does the opposite. It shows everything.

- **Sanding marks show.** White pigment settles into any scratch or swirl from the sander. The sanding has to be very even, especially where the edger meets the main floor.
- **Lap marks show.** If one pass dries before the next one meets it, you can see the line where they overlap. I work in a set order, with a wet edge, and fast.
- **Burn-through shows.** Between coats, the floor gets a light buff. Go a little too hard on a board edge and you can cut through the white, leaving a darker spot. That means another coat of NordicSeal before the finish.
- **The wood still matters.** White picks up the wood's undertones. Red oak goes a little pink, and some boards just take it differently than others.
- **It's hard to touch up.** You can't easily dab a little white on one spot and have it blend in. Fixes usually mean sanding and redoing a whole area.

None of this means you shouldn't do it. It means it's worth hiring someone who has done it before, and taking the time to look at samples on your own floor first. ## Which one is right for your home?

**Choose NordicSeal** if you want the white to last with very little upkeep. It's a good fit for busy family homes, kitchens and front halls, and for anyone who would rather sweep and mop than re-oil. The topcoat takes the wear, and the white stays put.

**Choose a one-step oil** if you love the feel of bare wood and don't mind regular care. Oils are great where you'd like to fix small spots yourself, without refinishing the whole room. Just plan on using the brand's own cleaning products, and on re-oiling worn paths every so often.

**Go natural or with a regular stain** if you want the most forgiving option. A clear finish or a medium stain hides small differences in the wood, and it's simpler to touch up later.
