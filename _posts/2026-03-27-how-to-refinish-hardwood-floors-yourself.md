Yes, you can refinish hardwood floors yourself. Lots of people try every year. But I get a steady stream of calls from homeowners who started the job, got halfway through a room, and realized it was a lot harder than the rental counter made it look.

I'd rather you know what you're getting into before you start. So here's what the DIY route usually looks like, where it tends to go wrong, and the exact steps I follow on every floor I refinish. If you still want to do it yourself after reading this, you'll at least know what good looks like.

## The DIY route: a rental sander and a palm sander

Most people who refinish their own floors start at the rental counter. [Home Depot's flooring rental page](https://www.homedepot.ca/tool-and-vehicle-rental/flooring-tool-rental) lists a few options: a square buff sander, an orbital floor sander, a drum sander and a 7" edger. The usual plan is to run the square sander over the main floor, then go around the edges and corners with a palm sander.

The square sanders are the friendliest of the bunch. They're hard to gouge a floor with, which is why rental shops push them. The trade-off is that they're slow. On a floor with old, thick finish, or a prefinished floor with its tough factory coating, you can spend a whole weekend going through sandpaper and barely get to bare wood.

Here's where the calls I get usually come from:

- **The finish won't come off.** The square sander has been running for hours and there's still shiny finish in every low spot.
- **The edges don't match the middle.** A palm sander can't keep up with a floor machine, so there's a dull, patchy band around the room.
- **Swirls and chatter marks.** You can't see them until the stain or first coat goes on, and then you can't unsee them.
- **Blotchy colour.** Stain soaks in unevenly when the grain hasn't been prepped properly (more on that below).
- **Dust everywhere.** Rental machines catch some of it. The rest ends up on your walls, in your vents, and in the finish.

None of that means you did something wrong. It means you're using homeowner equipment on a job that's built around professional equipment and a lot of hours of practice. Fixing a half-finished floor is very doable, but it often costs more than starting with a pro would have.

## How I refinish a hardwood floor, step by step

I'm happy to share exactly how I do it. Just know that the main machines below aren't available to rent anywhere around here that I know of, and each one takes real practice to run well.

1. **Sand the main floor with the Lägler Hummel.** The [Lägler Hummel](https://www.laegler.com/en/products/machines/hummel) is a big belt sander, and it's the most important tool I own ([more on it here](/our-main-sanding-machine)). It cuts flat and fast, but it's also unforgiving. Stop moving for a second with the drum down and it'll dig a dip into your floor. I make several passes, working with the grain.
2. **Edge the perimeter with a Bona edger.** My [Bona CombiEdge](https://www.bona.com/en-us/products/professional/machines/edgers/combiedge) reaches the strip along the walls that the big machine can't. My technique is to go around the room in one direction and work with the grain of the wood, so the edges blend into the field instead of leaving swirls.
3. **Repeat both steps through several grits.** How many depends on the floor. A site-finished floor with a worn coat might need three grits. A prefinished floor has a tough factory finish that's much harder to get off, so it usually takes more. Each grit takes out the scratches left by the one before it.
4. **Palm sand.** A smaller sander blends everything together and takes out any marks the edger left behind.
5. **Hand scrape the corners with a paint scraper.** No machine gets right into a corner or under a radiator pipe. I scrape those spots by hand with a sharp paint scraper, pulling it with the grain to take the old finish down to bare wood, then sand them smooth.
6. **Vacuum and prep for finish.** Every bit of dust comes up: floors, baseboards, window sills. Any dust left behind ends up stuck in your finish.
7. **Water pop the grain.** I mist the bare wood evenly with water from a [garden pump sprayer](https://www.homedepot.ca/product/hdx-3-8l-pump-sprayer-with-tank-gardening-tool/1001896232) to raise and open up the grain so stain soaks in evenly. **Don't skip this step.** I see painters who offer to do stairs and leave it out, and the stain ends up blotchy. Then they call me in to fix it, which means sanding it all off and starting again.
8. **Apply the finish.** Oil-based finish is more forgiving to work with, but the VOCs are terrible and your house will smell for days. Most products are water-based now, and they dry fast, so you have to work quickly and keep a wet edge. A roller is easier to control and lays down an even coat. A squeegee spreads finish faster over a big area, but it takes practice to avoid streaks and puddles. Whichever you use, the room needs a steady, warm temperature and good airflow while it dries.
9. **Buff between coats.** Once a coat is dry, I buff it lightly to knock down raised grain and dust nibs, then vacuum again. This is what makes the final coat feel smooth.
10. **Apply the final coat.** The last coat goes on the same way, with the same care for temperature and airflow.
11. **Wait for it to dry.** Water-based finish is usually dry enough for socks within hours, but it takes days to fully cure. Furniture and rugs have to wait. Rushing this step is how a brand new finish gets scratched in its first week.

That's 11 steps, and on a typical job, several of them get repeated three or four times.

## Keeping it dustless

Every machine I use, from the Hummel to the edger to the palm sander, hooks up to a high-powered dust extractor. I use the [Bona DCS 503](https://www.bona.com/en-us/products/professional/machines/dust-containment/bona-dcs-503). It pulls the dust straight off the sandpaper and into a sealed container, so it never gets into the air. That keeps your house clean, and it keeps dust out of the finish. You can read more about how it works on my [dustless sanding page](/dustless-hardwood-floor-sanding/).

## So, should you refinish your floors yourself?

If it's a small room, the floor is in decent shape, and you're patient, a rental sander can get you there. Go slow, keep the machine moving, and don't skip the water popping.

For most floors, though, the DIY route ends up costing more time, more sandpaper, and sometimes more money than hiring a pro. The difference is in the equipment and the hours behind it.
