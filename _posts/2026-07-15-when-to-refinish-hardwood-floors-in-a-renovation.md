Here's a call I get more often than I'd like. "The floors look great, but the painter spilled something and it took the finish right off." Or, "The installers dropped a kitchen cabinet and put a big dent in one of the floorboards. Can you come back and fix it?" Those are the calls I dread most. Fixing a brand new floor means sanding through finish that was only just put down, sometimes cutting out a board, and blending the repair back in. Somebody ends up paying for the same work twice.

It's never anybody's fault, exactly. When you're running a big renovation, whether you're a general contractor or a homeowner managing it yourself, it's tempting to book the floors early and check them off the list. But hardwood refinishing should be one of the very last trades into the house. Book it too early and someone else's work ends up in your brand new finish.

## The order of trades in a renovation

Every job is a little different, but the order of trades in a renovation usually runs from the inside of the walls out to the surfaces you see. Here's roughly where the floors fit:

1. Demolition and any structural work
2. Rough-in for plumbing, electrical and HVAC
3. Insulation and drywall, including taping, mudding and sanding
4. Primer and paint on the walls and ceilings
5. Cabinets, countertops, built-ins and tile
6. Finish plumbing and electrical: fixtures, outlet covers, light fixtures, vent covers
7. **Hardwood floor [sanding and refinishing](/dustless-hardwood-floor-sanding/)**
8. Final paint on baseboards, trim and stairs, and touch-ups
9. New closet and room doors, then furniture and move-in

The floor comes after just about everything else because it's the surface everyone else walks on, kneels on, and drops things on. A freshly sanded floor is bare wood until the finish goes down, and it soaks up anything that lands on it.

## What needs to be finished before I sand

Before I start, the other trades should be done, not "mostly done." Here's what I need finished, and why:

- **Electrical.** Electricians are up and down ladders all day, and they often need to fish wires through the floor or cut in a floor outlet. Better they do it before the finish goes on.
- **Plumbing.** A leak or a dropped wrench on bare wood is a stain or a dent I can't fix without sanding again. Get the toilets, sinks and radiators in and tested first.
- **HVAC.** New ductwork can mean new floor vents cut through the hardwood. Those openings need to be cut before I sand, so the edges get finished with the rest of the floor.
- **Drywall.** Drywall dust gets everywhere, and drywall mud on a finished floor is a mess. Mud buckets, stilts and ladders also leave marks.
- **Cabinetry and countertops.** Cabinets are heavy and get slid into place. Countertop crews carry big slabs. I want the kitchen in before I finish the floor around it.
- **Tile.** Tile setters work on their knees with wet thinset and saws. The transition between tile and hardwood also looks best when I finish up to tile that's already set.
- **Wall and ceiling paint.** Painters drip, and rollers spatter. Get the walls and ceilings painted first.

If there's carpet over your hardwood, don't rush to pull it up. It's good protection while the other trades work (more on that below). Just lift a corner in a closet when I come out to quote, so I can see what's underneath.

## How to protect your floors during the renovation

Even if the floors are getting refinished at the end, it's worth protecting them while the other trades work. Light scratches and scuffs sand right out. Deep gouges, dents, paint, drywall mud, adhesive and water stains can mean sanding deeper or replacing boards. Every sanding takes wood off the floor that it doesn't get back, so the less damage I have to sand out, the more life your floor has left.

Here's what I'd recommend:

- **Leave the old carpet down.** If there's carpet over your hardwood, it's one of the best floor protectors there is, and it's free. Leave the carpet and pad in place until just before I come. Lift a corner in a closet when I come out to quote, so I can see the wood underneath.
- **Floor protection board.** For bare floors, most hardware stores sell rolls of thick, heavy-duty protection paper made for renovations. Ram Board is one common brand. It stands up to foot traffic, ladders and tools much better than thin paper, and it's breathable, so moisture isn't trapped against the wood.
- **Canvas drop cloths.** These are great for painters and for short jobs in one room. Canvas soaks up drips, and it doesn't slide around underfoot like plastic does.
- **Add a hard layer where it counts.** Where cabinets are going in, where tools get set down, or where trades are carrying heavy loads, put sheets of hardboard or thin plywood over the paper.
- **Sweep first.** Sweep or vacuum before you put anything down. Grit trapped under a cover gets ground into the floor every time someone walks on it.
- **Tape the seams, not the floor.** Overlap the sheets and tape them to each other. Tape stuck straight onto the finish can pull it off, or leave a residue I'll have to sand out.
- **Mats at the doors.** A good walk-off mat at each entry keeps a lot of dirt and gravel from coming in on people's boots.

A few things to avoid:

- **Plastic sheeting right on the wood.** It's slippery and it traps moisture against the floor. A spill that runs under plastic just sits there.
- **Thin rosin paper on its own.** The red or brown builder's paper is cheap, but it tears easily. If it gets wet, the dye can bleed into the wood. It's fine under a heavier layer, but not as your only protection.
- **Rubber-backed mats and rugs.** These can trap moisture and discolour the finish. Stick with mats meant for hardwood.

Once I'm done and the floor is finished, this all goes for anyone working after me too. Ask me before you cover a newly finished floor, and I'll tell you when it's ready.

## Should floors be refinished before or after painting?

Both. The walls and ceilings get painted before I sand. The painter comes back after I'm done for the trim.

The painter is the one trade that should come after me. Baseboards, trim and painted parts of the stairs get scuffed when I sand right up to them, so it makes sense to give them their final coat once the floor is finished. That's also the time for any touch-ups on the walls. Once the finish has had time to cure, the painter can work carefully on drop cloths, and I'll tell you when the floor is ready for that. If you're having your stairs done too, [here's how stair refinishing works](/hardwood-stair-refinishing/).

### New closet and room doors can wait

If you're putting in new closet doors or room doors, those can wait until after the floors too. I sometimes take room and closet doors off so I can sand and finish the floor fully underneath and through each doorway. If they're not hung yet, that's one less thing in the way.

## It's the same order for a home, a shop or a clinic

The trades change a little from job to job, but the floors still go near the end.

- **Opening a coffee shop.** There's a lot going on: plumbing for sinks and the espresso machine, electrical for equipment, a counter, display cases, maybe a health inspection to plan around. Get the counters and equipment in place first, then I'll finish the floor around them. Leave time in your opening schedule for the finish to cure before chairs and tables come in.
- **Starting a massage practice in a century home.** Older homes often need new wiring, plumbing and heating before they're ready for clients. Those century-home floors are usually worth saving, so let the other trades do their work first and bring me in to make them look their best.
- **Gutting an old house to live in.** On a full gut, there are months of trades through the house. Hold off on the floors until the drywall is up, the kitchen is in and the walls are painted. It's hard to wait when you're excited to move in, but it's worth it.

If other trades have to work after me, cover the floor with a breathable floor protector, not plastic sheeting or tape straight on the finish. Ask me first and I'll tell you what's safe for your finish.

## Call early, book late

You don't have to wait until the end to talk to me. Call early in the project for a quote, so you know what the floors will cost and can build them into your budget and schedule.
