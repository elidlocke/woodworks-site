"How much per square foot?" It's usually the first thing people ask me, and it's a fair question. You want to know if refinishing fits the budget before you have someone come out to look.

The honest answer is that it depends on the floor. Two houses with the same square footage can come out quite differently on price. But I can give you a ballpark, tell you what moves a job up or down within it, and share a few things you can do yourself to bring the price down.

These are rough 2026 numbers for Kitchener, Waterloo and Cambridge, in Canadian dollars, before HST. They're not a quote. Your price comes after I've seen your floor, in person or from photos.

## Full sand and finish: roughly $7 to $12 per square foot

A full [sand and finish](/dustless-hardwood-floor-sanding/) takes the floor down to bare wood and builds new finish on top. It's the job for finish that's worn through, grey traffic paths, deep scratches, pet stains, or a colour you're tired of.

Most full refinishing jobs land somewhere between $7 and $12 per square foot for a clear, natural finish. That's a wide range on purpose. The floor decides where you fall in it.

The bottom of the range is for the easiest kind of floor: one big, open, empty room, in decent shape, with a clear finish and nothing to repair. Most homes have a bit more going on than that, so most jobs land somewhere in the middle.

## Buff and coat: roughly a third to half the price

A [buff and coat](/hardwood-floor-buff-and-coat/) (also called a screen and recoat) doesn't sand to bare wood. I lightly abrade the existing finish and put a fresh coat on top. It's usually done in a day, and it costs roughly a third to half as much as a full sand, often somewhere around $2 to $5 per square foot.

The catch is that it only works on a floor that's still in good shape. If the finish is worn through to bare wood, or the scratches are in the wood itself, a new coat just seals the damage in. If I come out and the floor needs a full sand, I'll tell you, even if you were hoping for the cheaper job.

## What pushes the price up

### Stain or a whitewashed look

A natural finish goes straight on the sanded wood. A [stain](/hardwood-floor-staining/) adds a whole step, and it asks more of the sanding, since stain shows every swirl and missed spot. Adding a stain usually adds a couple of dollars per square foot, sometimes more.

The same goes for a [whitewashed look](/how-to-get-the-whitewashed-floor-look) or any other product that goes on before the finish. It's an extra coat, an extra day of drying, and extra care.

### The layout, not just the square footage

Square footage is only part of the story. My big sander covers open floor quickly. Edges, corners, closets, doorways and hallways are done with smaller machines and by hand, and that's where the hours go.

Picture 600 square feet as one open living room. Now picture the same 600 square feet as four bedrooms and a hallway, each with a closet and a doorway. The second floor has far more edge to sand and a lot more hand work, so it costs more, even though the tape measure says they're the same size.

### Repairs and new boards

Replacing damaged boards, or weaving in new flooring where a wall came out or where carpet used to meet the hardwood, is extra. Finding boards that match and feathering them in so you can't see the seam takes time. Here's more on [hardwood floor repair](/hardwood-floor-repair/).

## Stairs: priced per tread

[Stairs](/hardwood-stair-refinishing/) aren't priced by the square foot. I price them per tread. A staircase is a lot of edges and corners in a small space, and most of it is sanded and finished by hand, one step at a time. A few square feet of stair tread takes far longer than the same area of open floor.

The shape of the staircase matters too. Landings, winders (the pie-shaped steps where a staircase turns), and open sides with spindles all take more time than a straight run between two walls. Stain on the stairs adds a step, just like it does on the floor. If you'd like your stairs done along with the floors, count the treads and send me a photo from the bottom and the top.

## Costs you can avoid

Some of the time on a job has nothing to do with sanding. If you're handy and have a free weekend before I start, here are a few things you can do yourself to keep the price down. Let me know at the quote what you're planning to take on, and I'll leave it out of the price.

- **Pull up the carpet.** Take up the carpet and the underpad, then pull every staple and the tack strip around the edges. Wear gloves, because tack strip is sharp. A pry bar and a pair of pliers or a staple puller are all you need. Get every last staple, since one left behind can tear the sanding paper. If other trades are still working in the house, leave the carpet down until just before I come. It's good [protection during a renovation](/when-to-refinish-hardwood-floors-in-a-renovation).
- **Take off the baseboards.** Run a utility knife along the top of the baseboard to cut through the caulk and paint, so it doesn't peel paint off the wall. Pry it off gently with a flat bar, with a putty knife or a scrap of wood behind it to protect the drywall. Number the back of each piece so you know where it goes back, and pull the nails out through the back.

If carpet or baseboards aren't for you, that's fine. I'm happy to do it, and it'll just be listed in your quote.

One thing I don't do is move furniture. The rooms need to be completely empty before I arrive: furniture, rugs, and anything stored in the closets. If you need a hand, book movers or line up some friends ahead of time.

## Why I quote after seeing the floor

A price per square foot on a website can't see your floor. It doesn't know if there's a pet stain by the back door, if there's enough wood left to sand, or if the hallway has six doorways. I'd rather give you a real number up front than a low number that grows once the work starts.

If you're comparing quotes, make sure they cover the same things: stain or natural, how many coats of finish, stairs, repairs and carpet removal. That's usually where two prices that look far apart turn out to be closer than they seemed.
