Some floors I can bring back to looking brand new. Others have been sanded so many times there's nothing left to save. I can usually tell which within a couple of minutes of walking in the door, and most of the time it's good news. If you're looking at your own floors and wondering, "Can these be brought back to life, or do I need to start fresh?", here's how to find out before you spend a dime.

Whether your floors can be refinished comes down to three questions. Is it real wood? Is it solid or engineered? And how much wood is left above the tongue and groove? You can answer most of that yourself in about five minutes. Here's what I'd look at if I were standing in your house.

## First, make sure it's actually wood

Laminate and vinyl plank have gotten good. Once or twice I've had to get down on my knees to be sure. But neither one can be sanded. The wood you're looking at is a printed picture under a clear plastic layer, and a sander goes right through it.

Look across the room. If the same knot shows up on board after board, it's printed. Laminate tends to sound a little hollow and clicky when you walk on it. And if you can see the edge of a board at a vent or a doorway, laminate shows pressed fibreboard and vinyl shows plastic. If that's what you've got, it'll need replacing, and I'm not the guy for that job. If it's real wood, keep reading.

## Solid or engineered?

Solid hardwood is one piece of wood from top to bottom, usually 3/4" thick. Most of the older homes around Kitchener, Waterloo and Cambridge have solid oak or maple, and those floors are tough. Looked after properly, they can be sanded several times over their life.

Engineered hardwood is a thin layer of real wood glued onto a plywood base. That top layer, called the wear layer, is the only part I can sand, so its thickness decides everything. At 3 or 4 mm or more, it can usually be sanded two or three times. Around 2 to 3 mm, I'll give it one careful sanding. Under 2 mm, I won't put a sander on it at all. A buff & coat can still freshen it up, as long as the wear hasn't gone through the finish into bare wood.

If you've still got the box, or you know the product name, the wear layer will be in the specs. If not, the vent check below will tell you.

## Pull up a vent and have a look

This is the check I trust most, and it's an easy one. If you've got forced-air heat, lift one of the floor vents straight up out of its hole. Now you're looking at the cut edge of the floorboards, a bit like the rings on a tree stump.

Partway up each board, you'll see where the tongue and groove lock together. The wood above that line is the part that gets sanded. Everything below it is what holds your floor together, and I never want to get near it. If there's at least 1/8" of wood above the tongue, you've got a floor I can refinish. A new solid floor starts out with about 1/4" there, and each full sanding takes off roughly 1/32" to 1/16". So a quick look tells you a lot about how many sandings that floor has left. While you're down there, check whether the board is one solid piece or a thin top layer on plywood. That tells you if it's solid or engineered.

A floor that's been sanded one too many times gives itself away. You'll see nail heads on the surface, the edge of the tongue peeking through between boards, or slivers lifting along the edges. On an engineered floor, you'll see plywood showing through. If that's what you find, there isn't much wood left. Sometimes a buff & coat buys it a few more good years, and sometimes it really is time for a new floor. I'll tell you which.

No floor vents? The edge of the floor at a doorway or at the top of the basement stairs will show you the same thing. Either way, it's one of the first things I check when I come out for a quote.

## A few bad boards don't mean a new floor

Damage in one spot is a different story from wear across the whole floor. Light grey water marks often sand right out. The dark, nearly black stains from pet urine or a slow leak go deep into the wood, and no amount of sanding will get them out. Boards that are cupped or feel soft underfoot can mean the subfloor took a hit too.

That still doesn't mean tearing out the room. I can cut out the bad boards, weave in reclaimed or new ones, then sand and finish everything together so the repair disappears. It costs a lot less than a new floor. [Here's how a floor repair works](/hardwood-floor-repair/).

## If it's still under carpet

I get plenty of calls from people who want a quote on hardwood they haven't seen yet. I can make a pretty good guess from the age of the house, a vent, or a corner of carpet pulled back. But I can't promise you anything until the carpet's up.

Carpet hides a lot. Years of pet accidents soak through the pad and into the wood, and those dark stains don't show until the carpet's gone. It's also common to find beautiful oak in the bedrooms and living room, then plywood in the hallway or in a room someone added on later. Those spots need new hardwood woven in before the floor can be finished as one.

So if you're serious about refinishing, pull the carpet first, or have it taken out as part of the job. Then I can see exactly what you've got and give you a quote you can count on.

## Sometimes it doesn't need a deep sanding

If the finish just looks dull and scuffed, but the wood isn't grey, stained or worn bare, you may not need a full refinish. With a [buff & coat](/hardwood-floor-buff-and-coat/), I scuff up the old finish and lay a fresh coat on top. No wood comes off, so it's the right call for thin engineered floors and for solid floors that don't have much left to give.

It only works over a finish that's still in one piece, though. If you can see bare wood in the traffic paths, or there's old wax on the floor, a fresh coat won't stick, and you need a full [sand and finish](/dustless-hardwood-floor-sanding/). I'll be straight with you about which one your floor needs, even when it's the cheaper one.
